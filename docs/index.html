A solar farm's paperwork says one thing.
What did the satellites see?

Before anyone spends money on a solar asset — lending against it, buying it, insuring it, auditing it — there is a reported record: a capacity, a build year, an owner. Almost none of it has been checked.

This takes 5 of India's largest solar plants and tests that record against nine years of free satellite imagery. No site visit, no licence fee, no data purchase. Every threshold is published, and where the evidence runs out, the page says so instead of guessing.

One asset, start to finish

Kamuthi Solar Power Plant

The same five checks every other asset gets, shown in full so you can see what the method actually produces.

How green the ground is inside the footprint

Higher means more vegetation. A working solar farm should sit low and flat.

35 /100 risk score worth a look

The database reports 648 MW at Kamuthi Solar Power Plant, operated by Adani Power Limited. It gives no commissioning year, so there is no build date to test against.

The satellite record opens in 2017 with the site already developed, so the construction check cannot speak to this plant. That is reported as unresolved rather than scored as clean.

Greenness inside the footprint has risen from 0.20 to 0.41 over the record (+0.0233 a year). On a working solar farm this should stay low and flat; a rise means vegetation establishing between and under the panels, which shades them at low sun angles and shows up as a maintenance cost before it shows up as lost output.

Daily maximum temperature averages 33.7°C, which implies roughly 14.0% of output lost to cell temperature at full sun. It only passes 40°C on 6 days a year, so the heat check stays clear. Physics rather than a fault, but it belongs in a yield assumption.

On water: standing water has covered part of the footprint 14% of the months on record since 1984, and it sits 7.7 m above the nearest drainage line, which is comfortable. That is enough to raise the flood check.

Net: 2 of five checks flagged (vegetation encroachment, flood exposure), giving a risk score of 35 out of 100 — worth a look.

What the risk score is made of

Five checks, each weighted by how much it would change a decision. A flag adds its weight; the total is the score.

0–24 little to investigate 25–54 worth a look 55–100 investigate before committing

A high score is not a bad asset. It means the public record and the satellite record disagree, or the site carries a physical exposure worth pricing. It ranks what to examine. It is not a valuation, a yield estimate, or a statement about any operator.

Where this run is thin

5 of 5 assets have no commissioning year in the source database, and all of them were already built when the satellite record opens in 2017. So the construction check — the heaviest weight in the score — could not be resolved for any asset here. Those are reported as unresolved, not as clean, and every risk score on this page should be read as a lower bound.

This is a finding about the public record, not a fault in the assets. It is also the clearest argument for the method: the gap is visible instead of silently filled in.

Five things this gets wrong — and why they are published

Any screening tool can be made to look certain. This one lists its own failure modes, because a number you cannot challenge is a number you should not rely on.

  1. The footprint is a circle, not the panels. Area comes from capacity at 2.2 hectares per MW. It takes in surrounding land, which dilutes every area measurement — most of all on smaller plants.
  2. Flood exposure is a terrain proxy. Height above the nearest drainage line is estimated from an elevation model, not modelled hydraulically. It finds sites worth a flood study; it is not one.
  3. The weather data is coarse. ERA5-Land cells are about 9 km across, so sunlight and temperature are regional, not measured at the fence line.
  4. Land classification confuses things. Bare earth, quarries and new industrial land all read as developed. Seasonal vegetation moves the signal enough to mask a real construction ramp.
  5. The source database is incomplete. Commissioning years are patchy. A timing flag is at least as likely to mean the database is stale as anything about the asset.

And the standing rule: a flag is a reason to look, never a finding. Nothing here is evidence of misreporting by any operator, and nothing here is investment advice.
